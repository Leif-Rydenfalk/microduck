1How to read this

This is the document a shop executes. Every number in it is either the slicer's own output, a measurement taken off the geometry, or a figure quoted from a named source — and where none of those exist the row says CANNOT DETERMINE and names the test that would settle it. No default has been substituted for a missing value anywhere in this file.

Three verdicts. PASS the measurement was taken and it clears the gate. CANNOT DETERMINE nobody has measured it; the row says what would. FAIL it was measured and it does not clear. A PRINTABLE-WITH-CARE part prints — it needs the named intervention, and shipping it without that intervention is the defect.
Where the numbers come from. Grams and seconds are BambuStudio's own result.json figures through ce-slice, never derived from volume [SLICE]. Overhang and wall are measured off the exact STL that was sliced, by tools/measure_dfm.py [DFM] — ce-cad has no overhang tool, and cecad.printed.printability says in as many words that it "does not guess at overhangs", so §3 is the measurement that was missing. Costs are computed here from those numbers against the rate brackets in §2.1; change a rate in tools/data/playbook.json and every table below moves.

2Process selection, part by part

Print or mould is not one decision, it is thirty. Each part has its own piece cost and its own tool cost, so each has its own break-even quantity — and for most of this robot that quantity does not exist, because the printed piece already costs less than the cheapest end of the sourced moulded piece price.

The measured answer, and it is not the one docs/production/process.md §8 reached. Of 30 printed parts, 18 have no break-even at all against the cheap end of the moulded piece price, and 30 have none against the dear end — the printed piece is simply cheaper. Where a break-even does exist it is 1,124 to 28,138 units at the Protolabs tool floor, and 7,521 to 188,212 units at the aluminium ceiling. §8 of process.md recommended moulding the nine visible parts at 1,000 units; it made that call with Σ hours marked CANNOT DETERMINE. With the hours measured, the arithmetic no longer supports it at 1,000. What can still move those nine parts to a mould is surface finish and throughput, not unit cost — and neither has a written acceptance standard yet (§8).

The case for a tool here is the layer line on a shell, and §8 records that nobody has written the acceptance standard that would settle it.
What a mould would cost that is not in the price. The geometry was drawn for printing — 1 mm plates, 2 mm walls, printed-style bosses, bearing seats, M2 counterbores. A moulded version needs draft, uniform walls and side-actions for the bearing bores and the head dome’s undercuts, at £1,000–2,000 per cam [PL4]. That is a redesign, not a re-quote, and it is not costed anywhere in this document.

3.2 The five recurring risks — the drawing-note block

  1. Small tapped holes do not print to size. Every Ø1.55/Ø1.60 (M2) and Ø2.05–2.30 hole prints undersize and, when horizontal, bridges. Drill to tapping size and form-tap after printing; heat-set inserts preferred for M2 into PLA [DFMMD] [PARTS5].
  2. Horizontal bores that carry a bearing or a press fit must be reamed — ankle Ø14/Ø15, upper-leg Ø5.37, bearing-roll / rigidity-plate / trunk-base Ø19, shin and hip pivots. A bridged bore is oval as well as undersize at the top [DFMMD].
  3. Thin flat plates must be laid flat — upper-leg-rigidity-plate at 1 mm and neck-plate at 2 mm. Standing either on edge fails. The overhang table above agrees: their best build directions are the flat ones.
  4. Sub-0.80 mm walls are knife-edges, not walls. They slice fine. The two genuine two-perimeter floors are power-support and hip-bracket at exactly 0.80 mm: no margin, so a 0.4 mm nozzle and ≥ 2 perimeters are not optional there [DFMMD].
  5. power-support’s sprung latch tongue is the one genuinely fragile, moving printed feature on the robot — thin, cantilevered and cycled. Highest wall count, print with the flex plane in-plane, and it is a gate at S1 [DFMMD].

5.2 The bearing rule this repo had wrong

“Press the outer race only” is wrong for half of this robot’s bearings. RELEASE.html §4 and the construction manual both give that rule. It is right for a seat that is a bore and wrong for a seat that is a boss, and this robot has both — read off the connection records themselves [CONN22] [CONN15]:
  • Bore seats (the Ø22 pocket in trunk_base for hip-yaw, and the head for head-roll; the ankle’s Ø15.0 × 2.3 pocket with its Ø16 × 0.5 lead-in) take the outer-ring pusher.
  • Boss seats (yaw2roll yaw_bearing_seat Ø16.0 × 1.95, hip-bracket roll_/pitch_bearing_seat Ø16.0 × 1.95, yaw-roll-motion roll_bearing_seat Ø16.0 × 4.0, and the shin’s Ø10.0 × 3.2) take the inner-ring pusher.
The load must pass through the ring that is being interference-fitted. Push the wrong ring and the press force crosses the balls — the bearing is brinelled, it still spins on the bench, and it fails months later in a customer’s hands. That is why there are four pushers in §5.1 and not two, and why S2’s first instruction is to read the seat before picking up a tool.

5.3 Torque

Three joint families, three CANNOT DETERMINE, three tests. Nothing here is a plausible default, and the one hard number that exists is listed only so that nobody uses it.

M2 screw into the XL330's own Ø1.6 x 6.0 tapped holes (horn face and case), 4 per horn / 8 per case face

CANNOT DETERMINE — ROBOTIS publishes no tightening torque for this part. Four searches over robotis.us, docs.robotis.com, emanual.robotis.com, the XL-series TDS PDF and forum.robotis.com returned specification and dimension pages only; none states a torque. The case material is not stated either (part:xl330-m288-t cad/part.py: MATERIAL = "PA" with the note that the vendor's case material is not on the fetched page), so it cannot even be derived.

Do not use: The DIN 912 class 12.9 chart figure for M2 — 0.60 N·m self-colour, 0.43 N·m zinc-plated, stress area 2.07 mm², tensile load 2530 N (HILLCO). That is a steel screw into a STEEL nut and is far above what a moulded polymer boss or a printed pilot will hold. Using it will strip the servo.

The test that settles it: TORQUE-TO-STRIP, servo case. 5 sacrificial XL330s × 4 holes. Calibrated 0-0.5 N·m screwdriver, M2×6 PHS TAP as shipped with the servo (S42). Drive each screw until the thread yields; record peak. Assembly torque = 0.50 × the LOWEST peak of the 20, rounded down to the driver's scale division. Re-run whenever the screw source changes.

Until then: Until that test is run the line uses a slip-limiting screwdriver set to its lowest usable setting and the written instruction is 'seat, then one eighth of a turn' — recorded as an unmeasured practice, not a specification.

M2 screw self-tapped into a printed PLA pilot (Ø1.55-1.65 measured on Pollen's meshes, connection:threaded-m2 joins.b)

CANNOT DETERMINE — No source read gives a strip torque for M2 in FDM PLA. The number is not a material constant either: it depends on the pilot diameter as printed, the boss wall, the layer direction across the thread and the print temperature — all of which are outcomes of the specific machine and profile in §4, not of the model.

Do not use: Any steel-fastener chart, and any figure quoted for injection-moulded PLA — an FDM boss fails between layers, not through the thread.

The test that settles it: TORQUE-TO-STRIP, printed coupon. Print 10 coupons on the production profile (§4) carrying the same Ø1.60 x 4.0 blind pilot as power-support, in the same build orientation as the real part, because the layer direction is the variable. Drive an M2×6 to failure; record peak and failure mode (thread shear vs boss split vs delamination). Assembly torque = 0.50 × the lowest of the 10. Publish the histogram, not the mean.

Until then: Same slip-limited driver. docs/DFM.md's standing instruction applies first: these holes print undersize, so drill to tapping size before any screw goes in.

M2 screw into a brass heat-set insert installed in a printed PLA boss (SPEC §6 uses inserts wherever a thread is loaded)

CANNOT DETERMINE — No readable manufacturer figure. Tappex's own "ETP51 General Performance Data" PDF was fetched and its text is not extractable; Prusa publishes the M2 insert's dimensions and no performance data; Markforged and the insert-guide pages give qualitative guidance only (TAPPEX). Nothing states a torque-out or pull-out load for M2 in PLA.

Do not use: The insert's own thread rating. The joint fails at the insert-to-plastic interface, not in the M2 thread, so the screw's rating is irrelevant.

The test that settles it: TORQUE-OUT and PULL-OUT, insert in a printed boss. 10 coupons per boss geometry, insert installed with the production soldering-iron tip and depth stop. (a) torque-out: drive an M2 screw until the insert spins in the plastic; record peak. (b) pull-out: tensile test to insert extraction; record load. Assembly torque = 0.50 × the lowest torque-out. Both numbers go on the drawing note.

Until then: Inserts are set with a temperature-controlled iron and a depth stop so the flange finishes flush; a proud insert is a reject at the station gate, because it means the boss did not melt and the interface is the weak one.

The only sourced M2 figure in this repository is a DIN 912 class 12.9 seating torque into a steel nut: M2, stress area 2.07 mm², tensile load 2530 N, 0.60 N·m self-colour and 0.43 N·m zinc-plated [HILLCO]. It is quoted here as the ceiling that proves the point: a printed PLA boss will not see a tenth of it before it splits.

5.4 Stations

S0 · Incoming inspection — batch, not per unit

In: XL330 ×15, bearings 22×16×4 ×11 and 15×10×3 ×3, NP-F550, Radxa Zero 3W, the three custom PCBs, camera, ToF, speaker, fasteners

  1. Count and identify every line against docs/BOM.md. A part with no MPN does not enter the line.
  2. Bearings: check OD and bore on a sample of 5 per lot against the measured envelope — 22.000/16.000 and 15.000/10.000 with the 0.2 bore and 0.3 OD chamfers (CONN22, CONN15). A bearing outside that envelope will not seat in a printed seat that was modelled to it.
  3. Servos: power each XL330 on the bench before it reaches S3 and read its model number and firmware. A servo that does not answer never gets built in.
  4. Battery: read the Wh marking on the case and record the lot. A pack with no Wh marking cannot be shipped (S12) and must not enter stock.

S1 · Print and post-process — plate-batched, off the unit clock

In: two plates: PLA 32 pieces, TPU 4 pieces (§4)

  1. Run the plates on the profile in §4. Supports ON — eleven parts carry a slicer warning and the stock 0.20 mm Standard preset does not generate them.
  2. Remove supports; deburr the first layer; check every part against its row in §3 for the named risk.
  3. Ream every bearing seat and pivot bore named in §3. FDM holes print 0.1-0.3 mm undersize (PARTS5) and a bridged bore is oval as well as small.
  4. Drill and tap, or heat-set, every M2 hole. Do not expect a threadable hole off the bed.
  5. Weigh the plate. A plate more than 5 % off the sliced total in §4 means the profile or the filament changed.

S2 · Bearing seating — 14 bearings per robot

In: 11 × 22×16×4, 3 × 15×10×3, the four pushers and the anvil from §5.1

  1. Read the seat off §5.2 FIRST: a BORE seat takes the outer-ring pusher, a BOSS seat takes the inner-ring pusher. They are not interchangeable and using the wrong one drives the load across the balls.
  2. Set the part on the anvil with the seat over its Ø24 through bore.
  3. Press square and steadily with an arbor press or a vice. Do not tap a bearing in with a hammer — a printed seat has no yield to absorb the shock.
  4. Spin the bearing by hand after seating.

  5. Label the servo with its ID before it leaves the station.

  1. Build the daisy chain in order: HAT → id34 (35) → id33 (60) → id32 (50) → id31 (165) → id30 (35) → imu200 (120), then it splits to id20…id24 and id10…id14.
  2. The 165 mm run crosses head-yaw and head-pitch; route it at the servo flank so it pays out the full ±170° sweep. A short-routed cable here is the classic failure that appears only after a hundred cycles.

  4. Sweep every joint through its full range BEFORE any shell closes and watch the loom.
  5. Close the shells.

7.1 What the box has to be

  1. A rigid outer box that passes the stacking test (PI 967 II) and, where spares are packed with it, the 1.2 m drop (PI 966 II).
  2. An insert that physically prevents the pack from making contact, or the pack ships unseated: the robot has no power switch anywhere in the sources, so nothing else prevents unintentional activation (S9 E.08).
  3. One face clear for a 100 × 100 mm battery mark, reducible to 100 × 70 mm, with a red hatched border ≥ 5 mm and the UN number ≥ 12 mm high (S9 F.01-F.02).
  4. Wh marking legible on the battery case itself — checked at S0, not at S10 (S12).
  5. USPS international takes ONLY batteries installed in the equipment, and forbids markings that identify the contents as lithium batteries — the opposite of the air rule. A store that mails internationally needs a different box artwork from one that ships by air freight.
  6. Box dimensions, board grade, print and insert design: CANNOT DETERMINE — nothing is published and no unit has been delivered. Pollen's Shopify shipping weight for a robot is 1200 g against a 780 g robot, so 420 g is box + controller + cable + insert.
The robot has no power switch. Nothing in any source gives it one. The packaging insert is therefore the only thing standing between a battery in a box and IATA E.08’s requirement that equipment be “packaged in a manner that prevents unintentional activation” [S9]. That makes the insert a regulated part, not a cosmetic one — and it is the single packaging item with no design and no supplier in this repository.
